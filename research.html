The research

Know the facts. Change the conversation.

Understanding anxiety and stigma gives us a better way to support friends. These research-informed ideas explain why asking for help should never be treated as weakness.

Explore the sources
01 / Understand

Three facts worth knowing.

Before deciding what to say, it helps to understand what the other person may be facing.

01 / ANXIETY

Being worried is not the same as having a disorder.

Feeling anxious sometimes is part of life. Anxiety disorders involve more than occasional worry and can interfere with everyday activities, school, and relationships. [1]

Key idea: Never diagnose someone based on one conversation.
02 / STIGMA

Labels and assumptions can cause harm.

Mental health stigma includes negative attitudes, stereotypes, and beliefs about mental health conditions. The CDC encourages empathy, acceptance, and language that avoids reinforcing stigma. [2]

Key idea: The words we use can reinforce or challenge stigma.
03 / SEEKING HELP

Fear of judgment can delay support.

Stigma can prevent or delay people from seeking mental health care. [2] The CDC recommends showing concern and listening so people feel heard and supported. [3]

Key idea: Listening can be a meaningful first step.
02 / See the connection

How stigma can keep someone quiet.

Imagine a student whose worry is affecting sleep and schoolwork. They might want support, but fear being judged.

This is an illustrative example, not a claim that every student reacts in exactly the same way. It draws on guidance about anxiety and barriers to seeking care. [1] [2]

03 / Put it into practice

Change the words. Change the tone.

The CDC recommends giving someone your attention, asking open-ended questions, and showing concern and support. [3] These original response examples apply that guidance and the CDC's advice on reducing stigma. [2]

Instead of

“You're just overreacting.”

Try

“That sounds difficult. Want to talk about it?”

This recognizes the person's feelings.
Instead of

“Only weak people need counseling.”

Try

“Asking for help takes courage. Want me to go with you?”

This challenges stigma and offers practical support.
Instead of

“I know exactly how to fix this.”

Try

“Would you like me to listen, or help you find someone to talk to?”

This gives the other person a choice.
04 / Know your role

Being a good friend doesn't mean being a therapist.

Friends can listen, check in, and encourage someone to speak with a school counselor, parent, teacher, or other trusted adult. If someone is unsafe or in immediate danger, get help right away rather than promising secrecy.

In the United States, call or text 988 for mental health crisis support. Call 911 when there is immediate danger.
